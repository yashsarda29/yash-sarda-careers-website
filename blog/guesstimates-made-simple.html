Guesstimates made simple: a 4-step method.

Nobody expects you to know the exact answer. They want to see how you think. Here's a method you can use on any guesstimate.

Yash SardaMBA, Great Lakes Chennai · 5+ years in industry

"How many cups of tea are sold in your college canteen every day?" Questions like this, called guesstimates, come up in consulting, analyst, product and even some general management interviews.

The answer itself barely matters. What matters is whether you can break a fuzzy problem into logical pieces, make sensible assumptions, and reach a reasonable number calmly.

The pointWhat interviewers are testing

  • Structure: can you break a big question into smaller ones?
  • Judgement: are your assumptions reasonable, and do you say them out loud?
  • Comfort with numbers: can you do quick maths without panicking?
  • Common sense: do you notice when an answer looks wrong?

The methodFour steps, every time

  1. Clarify. Confirm what's being asked: which place, which time period, what counts. "Tea from the canteen only, on a normal weekday?"
  2. Structure. Choose an approach and say it: from the demand side (people × how often × how much) or the supply side (outlets × capacity).
  3. Estimate. Fill in each piece with round, sensible numbers, explaining each assumption.
  4. Sanity-check. Step back and ask whether the final number makes sense, and adjust if it doesn't.

Worked exampleCups of tea sold in a college canteen per day

Let's use the demand side: people on campus × share who buy tea × cups each.

StudentsAbout 3,000 students enrolled. On a normal weekday, say 70% are on campus: 2,100.
Of those, maybe 40% buy tea from the canteen: about 840.
Tea drinkers average around 1.5 cups a day: about 1,260 cups.
StaffAround 200 teaching and non-teaching staff. Say half buy from the canteen, 2 cups each: about 200 cups.

Total: roughly 1,460, so call it about 1,500 cups a day.

Sanity check: if the canteen is open for about 8 hours, that's under 200 cups an hour, or 3 to 4 a minute, with spikes at breaks. For a busy college canteen that sounds believable.

Say every assumption out loud. A reasonable assumption that's clearly stated scores better than a "correct" number pulled from nowhere.

MathsTips for clean maths

  • Round aggressively: 2,100 × 0.4 is close enough to 840; don't fight decimals.
  • Write numbers down as you go, so you never lose track.
  • Use simple percentages (10%, 25%, 50%) unless there's a reason not to.

AvoidMistakes that cost marks

  • Jumping straight to a number without a structure.
  • Staying silent while you think. Think aloud instead.
  • Over-complicating with ten segments when three will do.
  • Skipping the sanity check, and missing an answer that's ten times too big.

NextHow to practise

Do one a day for two weeks, out loud, timed at about five minutes. Good starters: daily metro riders in your city, pizzas delivered in your area on a weekend, or two-wheelers sold in a month.
