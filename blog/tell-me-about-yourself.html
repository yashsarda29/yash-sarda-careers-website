"Tell me about yourself": a 60-second answer that works.

It's the first question in almost every interview, and the one most students answer by reading out their CV. Here's a better way.

Yash SardaMBA, Great Lakes Chennai · 5+ years in industry

"So, tell me about yourself." It sounds like small talk. It isn't. In the first minute of an interview, this answer sets the tone for everything that follows, and it often decides which questions you get next.

Most students either recite their CV from Class 10 onwards or freeze and say "I'm a final-year student from…" and stop. Both waste the best opening you'll get.

FirstWhy they ask it

The interviewer isn't asking for your life story. They want to know three things quickly: who you are professionally, what you're good at, and why you're in this room. They're also watching how clearly you can explain yourself, which is a skill every job needs.

The structurePresent, past, future

Use three short parts, in this order. It keeps you under a minute and sounds natural rather than rehearsed.

  1. Present: who you are right now, in one line. Your course, year and the one thing you're most focused on.
  2. Past: two pieces of proof. An internship, project, competition or responsibility that shows a relevant skill, ideally with a result.
  3. Future: why this role, and why it's the logical next step from what you just described.
Rule of thumbAim for 45 to 60 seconds. Short enough to hold attention, long enough to give the interviewer two or three things to ask you about.

ExampleA full answer for a fresher

Here's how it sounds for a final-year engineering student applying for an analyst role:

"I'm a final-year mechanical engineering student, and over the last two years I've moved steadily towards working with data."

"During my summer internship at a manufacturing firm, I analysed machine downtime data and built a simple dashboard that the shift supervisors started using every week. I enjoyed that far more than the design work, so I took up a data analysis course and did a project predicting demand for our college canteen."

"I'm applying for this analyst role because it's exactly that mix: working with real business data and turning it into decisions people use. That's what I want to build my career around."

Notice what it does: it explains a change of direction, gives two pieces of proof with outcomes, and ends by connecting everything to the job. It also invites easy follow-up questions about the dashboard and the project, which are topics you know well.

AvoidFive common mistakes

  • Reading out your CV. They have it in front of them. Pick the two most relevant points instead.
  • Starting with personal details. Hometown, family and hobbies can come up later if they ask.
  • Listing qualities without proof. "I'm hardworking and a team player" means nothing without an example.
  • Going on for three minutes. If you see the interviewer's attention drift, you've gone too long.
  • A memorised script. Learn the three points, not the exact words, so it sounds like you.

AdjustTailor it to the role

Your present and future parts stay similar, but swap the "past" examples for each interview. Applying for a sales role? Lead with the fest sponsorship you raised. A tech role? Lead with the project you built. Read the job description first and pick the two stories that prove what it asks for.

NextHow to practise

  1. Write the three parts as bullet points, not sentences.
  2. Say it out loud and record it on your phone. Time it.
  3. Listen back once. Cut anything that doesn't support the role.
  4. Practise with a friend who interrupts you with a follow-up question.
