Why your CV isn't getting shortlisted.

You're applying everywhere and hearing nothing back. It's rarely your profile. It's usually how the first page tells your story.

Yash SardaMBA, Great Lakes Chennai · 5+ years in industry

Most students assume a rejection means their profile isn't good enough. In practice, a lot of good profiles never get read properly. A recruiter screening a pile of applications gives each CV a quick first pass and decides in seconds whether to read further. Your job is to win those seconds.

Here are the six fixes I make most often when I review student CVs.

Fix 01Put your strongest proof in the top third

The top third of page one gets the most attention. If it's taken up by a long objective statement, your school marks and a list of hobbies, you've spent your best space on the least useful information.

Lead with what is most relevant to this role: your education line, then your best internship, project or achievement. Delete the objective statement. Nobody hires on "seeking a challenging role in a reputed organisation".

Fix 02Write results, not responsibilities

"Responsible for social media" tells the reader what you were supposed to do. It doesn't tell them what you achieved. Use a simple pattern for every bullet: action + what you did + result, with a number wherever you honestly can.

BeforeHandled social media for the college fest.
AfterRan Instagram for the college fest; grew followers from 800 to 3,200 in six weeks and drove 40% of online registrations.

No numbers? Use scale (how many people, how many items, how much time saved) or a clear outcome (shipped, adopted, won).

Fix 03Tailor it to the job description

One generic CV sent to fifty roles performs worse than five tailored CVs. Read the job description and underline the skills and words it repeats. If you genuinely have those skills, make sure the same words appear in your CV, ideally inside a bullet that proves them.

This matters twice over: the human reader is scanning for those words, and many companies use applicant tracking systems that filter on them before a human ever looks.

Fix 04Make it easy for software to read

Fancy templates with two columns, icons, skill bars and text inside images often get scrambled by tracking systems. Keep it simple:

  • One column, standard section headings (Education, Experience, Projects, Skills).
  • A clean font, consistent dates, and no tables or text boxes for important content.
  • Save as PDF unless the application asks for something else, and name the file properly: Firstname-Lastname-CV.pdf.

Fix 05Projects beat coursework

Listing every subject you studied says very little; every student in your batch studied them. One well-described project shows initiative, skill and the ability to finish something. For each project, cover the problem, what you built or did, the tools you used and what happened.

A recruiter isn't asking "what did you study?" They're asking "what can you already do?"

Fix 06One page, and proof one click away

As a student or fresher, one page is almost always enough. If it isn't, you're probably including things that don't help. Cut anything that doesn't support the role you're applying for.

Then make your claims checkable: add your LinkedIn, and a GitHub, portfolio or project link where relevant. Make sure the LinkedIn profile tells the same story as the CV.

Quick self-checkHand your CV to a friend for ten seconds, then take it back. Ask them what you're good at and what role you want. If they can't answer both, the top of your CV needs work.

What to do next

Pick one role you really want, apply these six fixes to a version of your CV built for it, and compare the response you get.
